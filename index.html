mesh vs. classic vpn

Why a mesh beats a single gateway.

A classic VPN sends everything through one doorway, wherever that doorway happens to be. A mesh lets each device talk straight to the one it needs. The difference shows up in speed, resilience, cost, and how much a single mistake can break.

THE PHYSICS, IN ONE EXAMPLE

Light in optical fibre covers roughly 200 km per millisecond, so distance sets a hard floor on latency that no hardware upgrade removes. Picture two colleagues in Frankfurt and a VPN gateway in Virginia, about 6,500 km away. A round trip between them must cross the Atlantic four times (out and back for each person), adding well over 100 ms before any processing happens. Connected directly, the same pair is limited by the distance between them — typically a few milliseconds or less. That gap is felt in every video call, SSH keystroke, file-share open, and database query.

Illustrative calculation from speed-of-light-in-fibre and great-circle distance; real routes are longer, so real hairpins are slower. Karst's own measurements are on the performance page and cover a single lab LAN only; relay-forced and multi-site paths have not been measured yet.

01

Shorter paths, lower latency

Traffic takes the direct route between peers instead of hairpinning through a central site. Nearby devices stay nearby, on the network as well as on the map.

02

No single choke point

Every conversation uses its own path, so throughput scales with the devices you add rather than with one gateway's uplink, CPU, and licence tier.

03

Redundancy built in

If one link, site, or relay fails, peers can reroute through another path instead of the whole network going dark. There is no one box whose outage takes everyone offline.

04

Graceful fallback

When NATs or firewalls block a direct connection, traffic flows through an encrypted relay. You can place relays close to your people so even the fallback path stays short.

05

Smaller blast radius

Classic VPNs often drop remote users onto a flat internal network. A mesh grants access device to device, per policy, so a compromised laptop can reach only what it was allowed to reach.

06

Less backhaul cost

Cloud-to-cloud and branch-to-branch traffic doesn't have to pass through, and be billed at, a central data centre. Fewer hops also means less bandwidth to buy and manage.

07

Grows without redesign

Adding a device means enrolling it, not re-planning tunnels, routes, and capacity at the hub. Multi-cloud, remote offices, and home labs join the same mesh the same way.

08

Control without the bottleneck

Coordination and policy live in a control plane you host yourself, while your data moves peer to peer. You keep the keys without sending every packet through your own servers.

PHREATIC + CONTROL

PHREATIC provides the post-quantum handshake and encrypted transport. karstd runs on each client; karst-control, the admin console, and the portal handle enrollment, identity, policy, audit, relay/TURN configuration, invitations, and device lifecycle. Subnet routing, exit nodes, ACL-gated SSH, metrics, and diagnostics are part of the beta feature set.

supported beta clients

One mesh, three desktop platforms.

We are testing the same Karst network across each supported client. Mobile and FreeBSD clients are not beta targets.

Linux

x86-64 and arm64. Debian/RHEL packages, systemd, desktop setup, TUN or userspace networking, and mesh DNS.

macOS

Apple Silicon and Intel. Signed/notarized packages, LaunchDaemon, menu-bar status, TUN or userspace networking, and mesh DNS.

Windows

x64. MSI, Windows service, userspace networking, and NRPT DNS. Wintun distribution and release-signing validation continue during beta.
