no networking background required

What a VPN actually does.

You don't need to know how packets work to decide whether you need one. This page answers two plain questions — what is a VPN, and what does it actually protect — using the situations people usually have in mind when they ask: a coffee shop, an ISP they'd rather not hand their browsing history to, and a trip somewhere they don't fully trust the local network.

question one

What is a VPN?

A Virtual Private Network makes a private, encrypted tunnel between your device and another point on the internet — in Karst's case, a server you or your organization runs. Once that tunnel is up, your device's traffic travels through it before going anywhere else.

Think of it as a sealed pipe laid across a public road. The road is still public. What's inside the pipe isn't.

question two

What does a VPN do?

Two things, specifically: it encrypts your traffic so whatever network you're physically connected to can't read it, and it hides your true destination and IP address from that same network, since all it can see is one encrypted stream going to your VPN.

Everyone downstream of the VPN — including the VPN operator itself — can still see where that stream ultimately goes. More on that below.

three situations

The same tunnel, three different reasons to want it.

A VPN solves one mechanical problem — hiding your traffic from whatever network sits between you and the internet. Who that network belongs to, and why you don't trust it, changes from one situation to the next.

☕

01

The coffee shop hotspot

Anyone else on that Wi-Fi — or whoever runs the router — sits on the same local network as you. Without a VPN, they can see which sites and apps you're reaching and how much data moves, even if they can't read the content itself. A VPN encrypts your traffic before it reaches their router, so there's nothing local to watch.

⌁

02

Not trusting your ISP

Your home or mobile provider sees every destination you visit by default — that's simply how routing works — and in many places it's legally allowed, sometimes required, to log or sell that history. A VPN moves that visibility from your ISP to wherever your VPN server sits, which is the whole point if you run that server yourself.

03

Traveling somewhere you don't trust

A hotel, airport, or national network abroad can watch, log, block, or redirect traffic the same way any ISP can — sometimes with a government looking at those logs. A VPN back to a network you trust keeps the local network from seeing your destinations, though some countries also try to detect and block VPN traffic itself.

content vs. metadata

HTTPS hides the page. It doesn't hide that you opened it.

"Content" is the actual page, message, or file — the thing HTTPS (the padlock in your address bar) already scrambles end to end. "Metadata" is everything around it: which site, when, from where, and how much data. HTTPS was never designed to hide that part, because the network still needs to know where to route your traffic.

HTTPS and a VPN protect different things, not competing ones: HTTPS protects content end to end regardless of network; a VPN protects metadata from whatever network you're physically sitting on. Neither one makes the other unnecessary.

the padlock, explained

A green padlock means “encrypted to someone my device trusts.” Not “safe.”

HTTPS encrypts the conversation between your browser and a server, and your browser checks that server's certificate against a list of certificate authorities it already trusts. When that check passes, the padlock appears. For almost every connection you make, that's a solid guarantee — nobody in between can read or tamper with the traffic.

WHERE THE PADLOCK CAN LIE TO YOU

The loophole is in "a list it already trusts." If something can add its own certificate authority to that list, it can sit in the middle, decrypt your traffic, read it, and re-encrypt it on its way to the real site — and your browser still shows the same green padlock, because as far as it knows the connection really is secure, just to the interceptor first. This is a documented, legitimate feature on many managed laptops and phones (often sold as security monitoring), and the same technique shows up on hostile hotspots that ask you to "install our certificate to join the Wi-Fi" and on some national networks.

A VPN genuinely helps with part of this: if your device's trust store hasn't already been tampered with, a VPN tunnel wraps your DNS lookups and TLS handshakes before they ever touch the local network, so a captive portal or airport Wi-Fi never gets the chance to offer a substitute certificate — there's nothing exposed for it to intercept. What a VPN cannot do is undo a root certificate that was already installed on your device beforehand, through a work profile or otherwise. At that point the interception is happening on the device itself, not on the network in between, and no VPN can see around that.

!
A VPN protects the network path. It can't protect a device that already trusts the wrong certificate.

If a "secure" site ever looks wrong, check your device's certificate or trust settings — especially on a work-managed phone or laptop. No VPN, Karst included, can see or override a trust decision your own device already made.

who you end up trusting instead

A VPN doesn't remove the need for trust. It relocates it.

Every row in the table above still has a last line: with a VPN, something sees your real destinations — it's just the VPN operator instead of your ISP or the local Wi-Fi. With most commercial VPNs, that's a company whose logging policy you're taking on faith. Karst is self-hosted, so that something is you, or your own organization, running infrastructure you already control — not a trade you're asked to make blind.
